Agriculture

Farming types, cropping seasons, major crops, reforms, globalisation and food security

🚜 Types of Farming in India
Farming in India varies with physical environment, technology and socio-cultural practices. It ranges from subsistence farming for the family's needs to modern commercial farming for the market.
🔥

Primitive Subsistence

"Slash and burn" farming on small patches with primitive tools (hoe, dao) and family labour. Depends on monsoon & natural fertility. Known as jhumming in NE India.

👩‍🌾

Intensive Subsistence

Practised in areas of high population pressure. Labour-intensive with high inputs and irrigation on small holdings. High output per unit area.

💰

Commercial Farming

Uses modern inputs — HYV seeds, fertilisers, pesticides. Crops grown for sale in the market. Commercialisation varies by region.

🌴

Plantation

A type of commercial farming — single crop grown on a large area (tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane). Capital-intensive, needs migrant labour, linked to industry.

Slash & burn has different names worldwide: Jhumming (NE India), Milpa (Mexico), Roca (Brazil), Ladang (Malaysia).
🌦️ Three Cropping Seasons
India has three main cropping seasons — Rabi, Kharif and Zaid — determined by the monsoon and temperature cycle.

❄️

Rabi Boost

Winter western disturbances (temperate cyclones) bring precipitation that helps the success of rabi crops in north-west India.

🌧️

Kharif Timing

Grown with the onset of the south-west monsoon. Rice-growing regions may raise three crops (Aus, Aman, Boro) in a year.

☀️

Zaid & Sugarcane

Zaid is a short summer season. Sugarcane, however, takes almost a year to grow.

🍚

Rice — Staple Food

Staple food of a majority of Indians. India is the second-largest producer after China.

🫘

Pulses

India is the largest producer & consumer of pulses. Being leguminous, they help restore soil fertility by fixing nitrogen.

🍵

Tea & Coffee

Plantation crops. Tea grown in Assam, WB (Darjeeling), TN, Kerala. Arabica coffee first from Yemen, grown in Nilgiri hills.

🧵

Jute — Golden Fibre

Used to make gunny bags, mats, ropes, carpets. Facing competition from synthetic substitutes.

🔬 Technological & Institutional Reforms
After Independence, reforms were needed as agriculture had been neglected. Land reforms, the Green Revolution and the White Revolution transformed Indian farming.
1950s–60s

Land Reforms

Abolition of zamindari, collectivisation, consolidation of holdings and "land to the tiller" to bring institutional change.

1960s–70s

Green Revolution

HYV seeds, irrigation, chemical fertilisers and machinery raised food-grain output — making India self-sufficient in food.

1970s (Operation Flood)

White Revolution

Boosted milk production through cooperatives — made India the world's largest milk producer.

Recent

Financial & Insurance Support

Kissan Credit Card (KCC), Personal Accident Insurance Scheme (PAIS), crop insurance, MSP, grameen banks and cooperatives.

💳

Kissan Credit Card

Provides affordable credit to farmers so they can buy seeds, fertilisers and equipment without falling into debt traps.

📻

Awareness

Weather bulletins and agricultural programmes on radio & TV help farmers plan and adopt better practices.

💵

Minimum Support Price

Government announces MSP and procures crops to protect farmers from a sharp fall in prices.

🌍 Agriculture & Globalisation
Globalisation is not new to Indian agriculture — spices and cotton textiles were traded worldwide even in ancient times. But modern globalisation brings both competition and opportunity.
Ancient

Global Trade Links

Indian spices, muslin and fine cotton textiles were exported to many countries, earning worldwide fame.

Colonial era

Exploitation

Under the British, cotton and indigo were exported as raw material, ruining Indian farmers and cottage industries.

1917

Champaran Movement

Gandhiji led farmers of Champaran (Bihar) against forced indigo cultivation by British planters.

Today

Post-1990 Competition

Indian farmers face competition from highly subsidised farm products of developed nations, squeezing profits.

⚠️ Challenge: Falling profits and rising costs push farmers to shift from cereals towards fruits, vegetables and flowers (horticulture) and to explore organic farming and genetic engineering.
🍚 Food Security & Sustainable Future
Agriculture provides a livelihood for about two-thirds of India's population and food for all. Its share in GDP is declining, but making farming sustainable and profitable remains vital.
👨‍👩‍👧

Livelihood

Supports ~2/3 of the population, provides raw materials to industry and employment to millions.

🤝

Bhoodan–Gramdan

Vinoba Bhave's "blood-less revolution" — landlords voluntarily donated land to the landless. Began at Pochampalli.

🌱

Organic Farming

Reduces chemical use, protects soil and health, and opens markets for chemical-free produce.

⚠️

Sustainability Concerns

Over-use of water and chemicals degrades land. Crop rotation, conservation and balanced inputs are needed.

Key Takeaway: Indian agriculture must become productive, profitable and sustainable — balancing food security for a growing population with the health of land, water and the farmers themselves.
