Forest and Wildlife Resources

Biodiversity, conservation, deforestation, community movements and Indian wildlife

🌍 India — A Mega-Biodiversity Country
India is one of the 12 mega-biodiversity countries in the world. It has about 8% of the total number of species in the world, estimated at 1.6 million. This is a huge diversity for a country with only 2.4% of the world's land area.
🌿

47,000+ Plant Species

15,000 flowering plants. 6th in world. Many endemic to Western Ghats, NE India and Himalayas.

🐾

89,000+ Animal Species

5-8% of world's amphibians, reptiles, mammals. 50,000+ insect species (many undiscovered).

🦅

1,200+ Bird Species

India is a birdwatcher's paradise. Includes endemic species like Great Indian Bustard.

🐟

2,500+ Fish Species

Found in rivers, coasts, coral reefs. Freshwater fish diversity especially high in Western Ghats.

🦎

Reptiles & Amphibians

King Cobra, gharial, saltwater croc, many endemic frogs in Western Ghats rain forests.

🌺

Endemic Hotspots

Western Ghats, Eastern Himalayas, NE India, Andaman & Nicobar Islands — high endemism.

📊 India's Forest Cover — Status & Types
India's forest cover is about 21.67% of the total geographical area (2021 assessment). The National Forest Policy (1988) targets 33% for plains and 67% for hilly/mountainous regions. We are far from these targets.

🐅 IUCN Classification of Species
The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) classifies species into categories based on the level of threat to their survival. India's wildlife includes species across all threat levels.
CategoryDefinitionIndian Examples
NormalPopulation is at a safe level for survival. No immediate threat.Cattle, sal, pine, rodents, common birds
EndangeredIn danger of extinction. Survival unlikely if threats continue.Black buck, crocodile, Indian rhino, Indian wild ass, lion-tailed macaque, sangai
VulnerablePopulation declining. May become endangered if conditions worsen.Blue sheep, Asiatic elephant, Gangetic dolphin, nilgiri langur
RareSmall population. Could become vulnerable/endangered.Himalayan brown bear, wild Asiatic buffalo, desert fox, hornbill
EndemicFound only in specific isolated areas. Naturally rare.Andaman teal, Nicobar pigeon, Mithun, Manipur bush quail
ExtinctNot found after extensive searches. No longer exists.Asiatic cheetah (1952), pink-headed duck, Sumatran rhino (India)
⚠️ Key Fact: According to IUCN Red Data Book, India has 44 critically endangered, 113 endangered, and 157 vulnerable species of plants and animals. The Asiatic cheetah was the last big cat to go extinct in India (1952).
⚠️ Causes of Forest & Wildlife Depletion
🌾

Agricultural Expansion

Largest single cause. After independence, vast forest areas converted to agricultural land to feed growing population. ~26,200 sq km lost (1951-80).

🪵

Commercial Logging

British era: massive felling for railways, shipbuilding. Post-1947: timber for construction, paper, furniture. Large-scale and often illegal.

⛏️

Mining

Open-cast mining destroys entire habitats. Doon Valley (Uttarakhand): limestone quarrying devastated forests. Sariska (Rajasthan): mining threatened tiger habitat.

🏗️

Large Dams & Projects

River valley projects submerged ~5,000 sq km of forest (1951-80). Narmada, Tehri dams displaced communities and wildlife.

🔥

Shifting Cultivation

Jhum (slash-and-burn) in NE India. Cycle shortened from 20-30 years to 3-5 years — no time for regeneration. ~1/3 of tribal area affected.

🐄

Overgrazing & Fuel Wood

India has ~500 million livestock. Grazing pressure on forests is enormous. 270 million tonnes of fuelwood collected annually — exceeds sustainable limit.

Historical Timeline of Deforestation

Pre-colonial

~40% Forest Cover

India had extensive forest cover. Tribal communities practised sustainable use.

1860s–1947

Colonial Exploitation

British exploited teak, sal for railways, ships. Forest cover fell to ~19%. Forest Act 1865 — government control.

1951–1980

Post-Independence Decline

26,200 sq km cleared for agriculture. 5,000+ sq km submerged by dams. Industrial and urban expansion.

1980–Present

Slow Recovery

Forest Conservation Act 1980 slowed diversion. Cover now ~21.67% but quality declining. Fragmentation is a major issue.

🛡️ Conservation of Forest & Wildlife
Conservation means protecting biodiversity while allowing sustainable use. India uses both in-situ (national parks, sanctuaries) and ex-situ (zoos, gene banks) conservation strategies.

Protected Area Network

🏔️

104 National Parks

Strictest protection. No human activity (grazing, farming, forestry) allowed. E.g., Jim Corbett, Kaziranga, Ranthambore.

🦌

566 Wildlife Sanctuaries

Limited human activity allowed. Grazing permitted in some. E.g., Bharatpur, Periyar, Chilika.

🌐

18 Biosphere Reserves

Large areas for conservation + sustainable use + research. E.g., Nilgiri, Sundarbans, Nanda Devi, Gulf of Mannar.

🐯

53 Tiger Reserves

Project Tiger (1973). Tiger population: 1,827 (2006) → 3,167 (2022). Major success story!

Key Legislation

1972

Indian Wildlife (Protection) Act

Banned hunting and poaching. Established protected areas. Created Wildlife Advisory Board. Comprehensive protection framework.

1980

Forest Conservation Act

Restricted diversion of forest land for non-forest purposes. Central government approval needed. Slowed forest loss significantly.

1986

Environment Protection Act

Umbrella legislation. Created the Ministry of Environment. EIA (Environmental Impact Assessment) made mandatory for large projects.

2006

Forest Rights Act

Recognised rights of tribal and forest-dwelling communities. Right to collect minor forest produce, habitat rights. Historic for tribal welfare.

✊ Community-Based Conservation
Some of India's most effective conservation has come from local communities — not just the government. People who depend on forests are often their best protectors.
🌲

Chipko Movement

1970s, Uttarakhand. Women hugged trees to prevent felling. Led by Sunderlal Bahuguna & Gaura Devi. Result: 15-year ban on green felling in Himalayas. "Ecology is permanent economy."

🦌

Bishnoi Community

Rajasthan, since 1700s. Protect blackbuck, chinkara, and khejri trees. 363 Bishnois sacrificed lives in 1731 (Amrita Devi). Still active today — filed cases against poachers.

🤝

Joint Forest Management

Since 1988. Government + community partnership. 84,000+ committees across 27 states. Communities protect forests; get access to non-timber products (fruits, herbs).

⛰️

Bhairodev Dakav Sonchuri

Alwar, Rajasthan. 5 villages declared 1,200 hectares as their own wildlife sanctuary. Self-imposed rules to protect wildlife. No government intervention needed!

🙏

Sacred Groves

Across India. Forest patches protected for religious beliefs. Preserve rare species. Found in Meghalaya, Rajasthan, MP, Maharashtra, Kerala. Ancient conservation wisdom.

🐯

Periyar Tiger Reserve

Kerala. Former poachers reformed into forest protectors and tourist guides. "Poacher turned gamekeeper" — community participation in anti-poaching operations.

Key Takeaway: Conservation succeeds best when local communities are active stakeholders, not just passive beneficiaries. The chapter emphasises that government laws alone cannot protect forests — community participation is essential.
